\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:technical}

This chapter introduces the technical background for this project.
The content is organised from foundational to specific: starting with
semantic segmentation, then covering deep learning fundamentals,
semi-supervised learning, self-supervised depth estimation, multi-task
learning and PAD-Net, and finally SDE-guided data selection,
geometry-aware mixing, and cross-task consistency learning.
Together these concepts form the foundation for the reproduction
experiments and architectural extensions described in later chapters.

\section{Semantic Segmentation}
\label{sec:semseg}

\subsection{Task Definition}

Semantic segmentation assigns a categorical class label to every pixel
in an image, producing a dense prediction map of the same size as the
input. Unlike image-level classification, which only answers what is in
the image, segmentation answers what is at every location in the image.
This pixel-level understanding is necessary in many real-world
applications. An autonomous driving system, for example, needs to know
which regions are drivable road, which are pedestrians, and which are
obstacles, rather than simply knowing that a car and a person appear
somewhere in the scene~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}.

Annotation precision also directly determines cost. Image-level
classification requires a single label per image, taking only a few
seconds. Semantic segmentation requires a human annotator to carefully
outline every object at the pixel level. In complex urban scenes with
dozens of object categories, this takes around 1.5~hours per image on
average~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}, adding up to over 4{,}460 working
hours for the full Cityscapes training set. This high annotation cost is
precisely what motivates research into training strong segmentation models
under limited annotation budgets.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}

Segmentation performance is measured by mean Intersection-over-Union,
abbreviated as mIoU. For a given class~$c$, IoU computes the ratio of
overlap between the predicted region and the ground-truth region:
\[
    \mathrm{IoU}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c + FN_c},
\]
where $TP_c$ is the number of pixels correctly predicted as class~$c$,
$FP_c$ is the number of pixels incorrectly predicted as class~$c$, and
$FN_c$ is the number of pixels that belong to class~$c$ but were
predicted as something else. The final mIoU is the arithmetic mean
across all classes.

The reason for preferring mIoU over simple pixel accuracy is that class
frequencies in urban driving scenes are highly imbalanced. Road and sky
pixels far outnumber pedestrians or traffic lights. A model that
predicts road for most pixels would achieve high pixel accuracy while
completely failing on rare but safety-critical classes. Because mIoU
computes a separate score for each class before averaging, rare and
common classes contribute equally, giving a fairer picture of overall
model performance.

\subsection{The Encoder--Decoder Paradigm}

Modern segmentation models generally follow an encoder--decoder
structure~\cite{long2015fcn}. The encoder extracts hierarchical features
from the input image by progressively reducing spatial resolution. As the
network goes deeper, each feature map location captures increasingly
abstract semantic information, but at the cost of spatial detail. The
decoder reverses this process, gradually restoring resolution and
combining high-level semantic features with low-level spatial detail from
the encoder to produce sharp, accurate boundary predictions.

In DeepLab-style models~\cite{chen2018deeplabv3plus}, two components
play a central role. Atrous convolution inserts gaps between convolution
kernel elements to expand the receptive field without increasing the
number of parameters or reducing spatial resolution, allowing each pixel
to incorporate context from a wider area. Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling
(ASPP) runs multiple atrous convolutions in parallel at different dilation
rates, capturing semantic information at multiple scales simultaneously.
This is particularly useful when objects in the scene vary greatly in
size, such as distant pedestrians alongside nearby buildings.

\subsection{U-Net Decoder and Skip Connections}
\label{sec:unet}

Even with ASPP aggregating multi-scale features, the spatial resolution of
deep feature maps has been substantially reduced by the encoder, and simple
bilinear upsampling alone struggles to recover precise object boundaries.
U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015unet} addresses this with skip connections: at
each upsampling step, the encoder feature map from the corresponding
resolution is concatenated directly onto the decoder feature map, bypassing
the intermediate compression stages.

As a result, the decoder simultaneously has access to two types of
information: high-level semantic understanding from the deep layers, and
low-level edge and texture detail from the shallow layers. Combining these
produces predictions that are both semantically accurate and spatially
precise.

In this project, the decoder chains ASPP with four U-Net-style upsampling
blocks. ASPP first aggregates multi-scale features from the deepest encoder
layer, and the four upsampling blocks then progressively fuse encoder
features from each corresponding resolution via skip connections, restoring
the spatial size to match the original input. Both the segmentation decoder
and the depth decoder share this same architecture. This design consistency
is important: decoder weights learned by the depth branch during
self-supervised pretraining can be used to directly initialise the
segmentation branch, transferring geometric priors to the segmentation task
without relearning the decoder structure from scratch.

\subsection{The Cityscapes Dataset}

All experiments in this project are conducted on the Cityscapes
dataset~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}. Cityscapes contains 2{,}975 training
images and 500 validation images from European urban driving scenes, with
pixel-level annotations across 19 semantic categories including road,
building, vehicle, and pedestrian classes. In addition, Cityscapes provides
30 unlabelled video frames surrounding each annotated image. These
unlabelled frames are not used for segmentation training, but are used in
this project for self-supervised depth estimation, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:sde}.

\section{Deep Learning Fundamentals}
\label{sec:backbones}

\subsection{Convolutional Neural Networks}

Convolutional neural networks are the foundation of modern computer
vision. Unlike fully connected networks, CNNs use convolutional operations
to extract features from local image regions. Shared convolutional filters
allow the network to detect the same patterns --- edges, textures, and
object parts --- at different locations in an image. As the network goes
deeper, these low-level features gradually combine into higher-level
semantic representations, such as vehicle shapes or pedestrian
silhouettes. This hierarchical extraction mechanism makes CNNs effective
for image understanding tasks.

\subsection{Batch Normalisation}
\label{sec:batchnorm}

A practical challenge in training deep networks is that the distribution
of activations within each layer shifts as the weights of earlier layers
are updated, forcing subsequent layers to constantly adapt to a moving
target. This phenomenon, known as internal covariate shift, slows down
training and makes it harder to use high learning rates.

Batch Normalisation (BN)~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} addresses this by
standardising the activations of each layer across the samples in a
mini-batch before passing them to the next layer. Concretely, for each
channel, BN computes the mean~$\mu_\mathcal{B}$ and
variance~$\sigma^2_\mathcal{B}$ of the activations over the current
training batch, and normalises:
\[
    \hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_\mathcal{B}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_\mathcal{B} + \epsilon}},
\]
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. Two
learnable parameters then rescale and shift the normalised value:
$y = \gamma\hat{x} + \beta$. This stabilises the training signal,
allows the use of higher learning rates, and generally reduces the
sensitivity of training to the choice of weight initialisation.

A well-known limitation of BN is that the quality of its statistics
depends on the number of samples in the mini-batch. With very small
batches, the estimated mean and variance are noisy, which can destabilise
training. In multi-GPU settings where each device only sees a fraction of
the full batch, Synchronised Batch Normalisation
(SyncBN)~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} resolves this by aggregating
statistics across all GPUs before normalising, so that the effective
batch size for statistics computation equals the total number of samples
across all devices. This distinction is directly relevant to this
project: because all experiments were conducted on a single GPU with
batch size~2, SyncBN was not applicable, and BN statistics were estimated
from only two samples per step---a constraint discussed further in
Chapter~\ref{chap:execution}.

\subsection{ResNet-101}

As CNNs became deeper, vanishing gradients became a serious obstacle.
During training, networks learn by computing gradients---signals that
indicate how each weight should be adjusted to reduce the prediction
error. In very deep networks, these gradients must propagate back through
many successive layers. At each layer, the gradient is multiplied by the
local derivative of the activation function. When these derivatives are
small (less than 1), repeated multiplication causes the gradient to shrink
exponentially, eventually becoming so small that the early layers receive
virtually no update signal---a problem known as the vanishing gradient.

ResNet~\cite{he2016resnet} addresses this with residual connections. Each
residual block has a standard convolutional path alongside a shortcut that
adds the block's input directly to its output:
\[
    \mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x},
\]
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the block's input, $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})$
represents the convolutional layers, and $\mathbf{y}$ is the output.
Because the shortcut bypasses the convolutional layers entirely, gradients
can flow directly from any layer back to the input without passing through
the intermediate multiplications, eliminating the vanishing gradient
problem and making stable training of very deep networks possible.

This project uses ResNet-101~\cite{he2016resnet} as the encoder backbone
throughout. With 101 layers, it provides strong general-purpose visual
feature extraction and is a standard choice in semantic segmentation
frameworks.

\subsection{ImageNet Pre-training and Transfer Learning}

Training a deep network from scratch requires large amounts of data and
long training times, and the results are often unstable. Transfer
learning addresses this by first training the network on a large dataset,
then reusing the learned weights as a starting point for a different but
related task. ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet} contains over one million
images across 1{,}000 categories. Networks pre-trained on it have already
learned to detect a wide range of general visual features, including
edges, textures, and object parts.

In this project, the ResNet-101 encoder is initialised with ImageNet
pre-trained weights before any task-specific training. Compared to
training from scratch, this leads to faster convergence and better
performance, especially when the amount of labelled data is limited.

\section{Semi-Supervised Learning}
\label{sec:ssl}

\subsection{Label-Efficient Setting}
\label{sec:label-efficient}

As noted in Section~\ref{sec:semseg}, manual annotation for semantic
segmentation is expensive, averaging around 1.5~hours per image. In
practice, fully annotating an entire dataset is often not feasible.
To systematically study model performance across different annotation
budgets, the field has established a standard evaluation protocol: a
fixed proportion of the training set is selected as the labelled subset,
and the remaining images are treated as unlabelled data.

This project runs experiments at three budget levels: 100~images
(approximately 1/30 of the training set), 372~images (approximately
1/8), and 744~images (approximately 1/4). These three levels are widely
used benchmarks in the semi-supervised segmentation
literature~\cite{hoyer2023ijcv_sde}, covering scenarios from extremely
scarce to moderately limited annotation. Among them, the 372-label
setting is the primary experimental regime for this project: all
ablations of the proposed components and the hyperparameter search for
the cross-task consistency loss are conducted at this level. The
100-label regime produces results with high variance across seeds, making
it difficult to reliably measure fine-grained improvements. The 372-label
setting retains the challenge of working with limited supervision while
providing a stable enough evaluation environment to draw meaningful
conclusions.

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Semi-supervised learning (SSL) aims to train a model using a small amount
of labelled data alongside a much larger pool of unlabelled
data~\cite{chapelle2006ssl}. In the context of semantic segmentation,
only a small subset of images carries pixel-level annotations, while the
rest remain unlabelled. The model must extract semantic knowledge from
the labelled samples while also exploiting structural information in the
unlabelled ones to improve generalisation.

\subsection{Pseudo-Labels and Confirmation Bias}

Pseudo-labelling is one of the most widely used strategies in
SSL~\cite{lee2013pseudolabel}. The model generates predictions for
unlabelled images, treats those predictions as labels, and then continues
training on the combined labelled and pseudo-labelled data. This process
can be repeated iteratively as pseudo-label quality improves alongside
the model.

However, pseudo-labelling carries a fundamental risk known as confirmation
bias. When the model's initial performance is poor, the pseudo-labels it
generates contain many errors. Training on those incorrect labels
reinforces the model's mistakes rather than correcting them, causing
performance to stagnate or even degrade~\cite{arazo2020pseudolabel}.
This problem is especially severe when labelled data is extremely scarce,
since the initial predictions are less reliable and the proportion of
erroneous pseudo-labels is higher.

\subsection{Mean Teacher}

To reduce confirmation bias, this project uses the Mean
Teacher~\cite{tarvainen2017meanteacher} framework to generate
pseudo-labels. Mean Teacher maintains two networks: a student that is
updated through normal gradient-based training, and a teacher whose
weights are updated via an exponential moving average (EMA) of the
student weights:
\[
    \theta_T \leftarrow \alpha\,\theta_T + (1 - \alpha)\,\theta_S,
\]
where $\theta_T$ and $\theta_S$ are the teacher and student weights
respectively, and $\alpha$ is a smoothing coefficient close to~1 (set to
0.99 in this project). Because the teacher weights are a running average
of the student's history, the teacher behaves as a temporal ensemble,
producing more stable and reliable predictions than any single student
checkpoint. These stable predictions are then used as pseudo-labels for
the unlabelled data, reducing the noise that would otherwise cause
confirmation bias.

\subsection{Consistency Regularisation, Data Augmentation, and Data Mixing}
\label{sec:consistency}

Consistency regularisation encourages the model to produce the same
prediction for a given image regardless of what perturbations are applied
to it~\cite{sohn2020fixmatch}. This pushes the model to learn
representations that are \emph{robust} to various transformations ---
meaning the model can still predict accurately even when the input has
been altered or distorted.

Data augmentation achieves this by applying random transformations to
training images, such as flipping, cropping, brightness adjustments, and
Gaussian blur. Exposing the model to many different-looking versions of
the same semantic content prevents it from relying on superficial visual
cues and encourages it to learn deeper, more transferable features.

Within the Mean Teacher framework, data augmentation operates through a
\emph{weak--strong augmentation pairing} strategy. For each unlabelled
image, two versions are created: a weakly augmented version, which
receives only mild transformations such as random horizontal flipping, is
passed to the teacher to generate reliable pseudo-labels; a strongly
augmented version, which additionally applies colour jitter, random
greyscale conversion, and Gaussian blur, is passed to the student for
training. The training objective requires the student's predictions on the
strongly augmented image to match the teacher's pseudo-labels from the
weakly augmented one. The teacher provides high-quality supervision from
images close to the originals, while the student is forced to match those
signals under more difficult conditions, which encourages it to learn
genuinely semantic features rather than surface-level patterns.

Data mixing is a complementary augmentation strategy. It combines the
pixels and corresponding labels of two images according to a binary mask,
creating a synthetic training sample containing regions from both images.
This forces the model to recognise the same class in different contexts,
improving generalisation. ClassMix~\cite{olsson2021classmix} builds the
mixing mask from class segments, but ignores the geometric structure of
the scene and can produce physically implausible composites --- for
example, pasting a distant building in front of a nearby car. DepthMix
resolves this by incorporating depth-based constraints, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:data-selection-mixing}.

\section{Self-Supervised Monocular Depth Estimation}
\label{sec:sde}

\subsection{Motivation}

Depth information is a natural complement to semantic segmentation.
Objects in a scene tend to have consistent depth profiles, and semantic
boundaries frequently coincide with depth discontinuities. Obtaining
ground-truth depth annotations, however, requires expensive equipment
such as LiDAR sensors and does not scale easily. Self-supervised depth
estimation (SDE) offers an alternative: it learns to predict depth purely
from unlabelled video sequences, using the geometric relationships between
consecutive frames as supervision, with no manual annotation
required~\cite{godard2017unsupervised}.

\subsection{View Synthesis and Photometric Loss}

The core idea behind SDE is view synthesis. If the depth of every pixel
in an image is known along with the camera motion between two frames, one
frame can be geometrically warped into the appearance of the other. If
the synthesised frame closely matches the real target frame, the predicted
depth and camera motion must be accurate.

In practice, SDE trains two networks: a depth network that predicts a
dense depth map from a single image, and a pose network that estimates
the relative camera motion between adjacent frames. During training, the
source frame is warped into the target view using the predicted depth and
pose, and the photometric reprojection error between the synthesised and
real target frames is minimised. This loss combines an $\ell_1$ pixel
intensity term with a Structural Similarity
(SSIM)~\cite{wang2004ssim} term~\cite{godard2017unsupervised} that
captures local structural consistency. Because the entire process requires
no annotations, SDE can be trained on large quantities of easily
accessible unlabelled video data, building up rich geometric priors that
subsequently benefit semantic segmentation.

\subsection{Depth Pre-training Variants: \texttt{fd0} and \texttt{fd2}}
\label{sec:fd0-fd2}

Within the framework of Hoyer et al.~\cite{hoyer2023ijcv_sde}, SDE
pre-training produces two encoder weight variants that differ in how the
encoder is regularised during depth pre-training.

\textbf{\texttt{fd0}} applies standard depth pre-training, optimising
the encoder purely to minimise the SDE photometric loss. While this
produces an encoder with strong geometric representations, it risks
drifting away from the ImageNet feature space: because the SDE objective
provides no incentive to retain general visual features, the encoder may
``forget'' the semantic knowledge acquired during ImageNet pre-training.
When these weights are later transferred to initialise the segmentation
branch, the lost semantic features can be difficult to recover, limiting
the benefit of the transfer.

\textbf{\texttt{fd2}} addresses this forgetting problem by adding a
feature-distance regularisation loss~$\mathcal{L}_F$ during depth
pre-training~\cite{hoyer2023ijcv_sde}. This loss penalises the
$\ell_2$ distance between the current encoder's bottleneck features
$f^E_\theta$ and those of the frozen ImageNet-initialised
encoder~$f^E_I$:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_F = \| f^E_\theta - f^E_I \|_2.
\]
The total pre-training loss becomes:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{D,\text{pretrain}} = \mathcal{L}_D + \lambda_F \mathcal{L}_F,
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_D$ is the standard SDE photometric loss and
$\lambda_F$ controls the strength of the regularisation. By keeping the
encoder features close to their ImageNet initialisation, \texttt{fd2}
retains general semantic representations while still acquiring geometric
knowledge from depth pre-training, making it a stronger starting point
for the downstream segmentation task compared to \texttt{fd0}.

In the experiments of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}, \texttt{fd0} is
used as the encoder initialisation for the \emph{Transfer (no
$\mathcal{L}_F$)} condition, and \texttt{fd2} is used for the
\emph{Transfer ($\mathcal{L}_F = \checkmark$)} and \emph{Multi-Task
($\mathcal{L}_F = \checkmark$)} conditions (Table~5). The author-released
checkpoints for both variants are used throughout and kept fixed across
all downstream experiments.

\section{Multi-Task Learning and PAD-Net}
\label{sec:mtl}

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning}

Multi-task learning (MTL) trains a single model on multiple related tasks
simultaneously by sharing representations across
them~\cite{caruana1997multitask}. In this project, semantic segmentation
and depth estimation are trained jointly. The shared encoder receives
gradient signals from both tasks and is guided to learn features that are
useful for both geometry and semantics. Compared to transfer learning,
where SDE pre-trained weights are used only to initialise the
segmentation model, MTL maintains the depth supervision signal throughout
the entire training process, providing a continuous source of geometric
supervision that is especially valuable when labelled segmentation data
is scarce.

\subsection{Attention Mechanism}

The attention mechanism allows a model to dynamically focus on the most
relevant parts of its input~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. Self-attention
lets every position in a feature map interact with every other position,
computing a weighted representation where the weights reflect pairwise
relevance between locations. This allows the network to capture
long-range dependencies --- for example, relating the appearance of a
distant vehicle to nearby road markings. In the context of multi-task
learning, attention can selectively transfer relevant information between
the feature representations of different tasks.

\subsection{PAD-Net}

The multi-task architecture used in this project is based on
PAD-Net~\cite{xu2018padnet}. PAD-Net maintains two parallel decoder
branches on top of a shared ResNet-101 encoder, one for depth estimation
and one for semantic segmentation. Both branches use the ASPP and U-Net
upsampling block structure described in Section~\ref{sec:unet}. After the
third upsampling block, the two branches exchange information through an
attention-guided distillation module: each branch first applies
self-attention to its own features, then incorporates the other branch's
attended features via element-wise addition:
\[
    \tilde{f}_\text{seg}   = f_\text{seg}   +
        \mathrm{SA}_\text{depth}(f_\text{depth}),
    \qquad
    \tilde{f}_\text{depth} = f_\text{depth} +
        \mathrm{SA}_\text{seg}(f_\text{seg}),
\]
where $\mathrm{SA}_\text{seg}(\cdot)$ and
$\mathrm{SA}_\text{depth}(\cdot)$ are the self-attention modules of the
segmentation and depth branches respectively, $f_\text{seg}$ and
$f_\text{depth}$ are the intermediate feature maps produced by each
branch at the distillation layer, and $\tilde{f}$ denotes the updated
features after cross-task information exchange. In plain terms: the
segmentation branch looks at what the depth branch has learned about
scene geometry and incorporates it into its own features, and vice versa.
The updated features $\tilde{f}$ are then passed through the remaining
decoder layers to produce the final predictions. This design allows each
task to selectively absorb geometric or semantic cues from the other
branch without directly coupling the final prediction heads.

\section{SDE-Guided Data Selection and Geometry-Aware Mixing}
\label{sec:data-selection-mixing}

\subsection{Active Learning and SDE-Guided Data Selection}

When annotation budgets are limited, randomly choosing which images to
label is not the most efficient strategy, since different images
contribute very differently to model training. Active
learning~\cite{settles2009activelearning} addresses this by iteratively
selecting the most informative samples based on the model's current
state, allowing a given budget to go further.

Traditional active learning for segmentation relies on the segmentation
model's own prediction uncertainty to guide selection, which still
requires a human annotator for each selected sample. Hoyer et
al.~\cite{hoyer2023ijcv_sde} replace the human annotator with SDE as a
proxy task. Diversity sampling (DS) uses SDE features to select images
covering a diverse range of scene content, while uncertainty sampling
(US) selects images where the current depth model is least confident.
Because depth estimation and semantic segmentation share similar notions
of sample difficulty, SDE-based selection signals transfer well to the
segmentation task without requiring any human involvement. Combining both
criteria (DS+US) consistently outperforms using either alone.

\subsection{DepthMix}

Once pseudo-labels are available from the teacher network, data mixing
uses them to build synthetic training samples. As described in
Section~\ref{sec:consistency}, ClassMix~\cite{olsson2021classmix}
constructs mixing masks from class segments but ignores scene geometry,
which can produce physically implausible composites.

DepthMix~\cite{hoyer2023ijcv_sde} resolves this by using SDE-predicted
depth to enforce geometrically consistent composition. When combining two
images, a pixel from the first image is copied to the composite only if
its predicted depth is smaller than that of the corresponding pixel in
the second image. In other words, only objects closer to the camera can
occlude objects further away, consistent with how the real world works.
This produces synthetic samples that are more physically plausible and
better suited for learning accurate object boundaries.

The three components --- data selection~(S), DepthMix~(DX), and
multi-task learning~(MTL) --- target distinct stages of the learning
pipeline: data quality, augmentation consistency, and representation
learning respectively. Because they address complementary bottlenecks,
combining them has the potential to yield improvements beyond what any
single component achieves on its own. Their individual and joint
contributions are quantified in Chapter~\ref{chap:execution}.

\section{Cross-Task Consistency Learning}
\label{sec:cross-task}

In multi-task learning, different task decoders share an encoder but
learn their own task-specific feature representations independently,
with no direct constraint on how their intermediate features relate to
each other. Cross-task consistency learning addresses this by explicitly
encouraging features from different tasks to reflect compatible structural
information, since both sets of features are extracted from the same
scene and should therefore be geometrically and semantically compatible
at intermediate representations.

Zamir et al.~\cite{zamir2020crosstask} formalise this idea through
inference-path invariance: predictions obtained via different combinations
of tasks should remain geometrically consistent. Nakano et
al.~\cite{nakano2021crosstask} apply a similar principle to multi-task
learning by directly constraining intermediate features across tasks to
improve performance. Li et al.~\cite{li2022mtl} extend this further to
settings with partial annotations, where different tasks may have access
to different amounts of labelled data.

This last point is directly relevant here. The depth decoder receives
self-supervised training signals from all available unlabelled frames,
while the segmentation decoder is only supervised on the small labelled
subset. This asymmetry means the depth branch accumulates richer
geometric representations than the segmentation branch. By explicitly
aligning their intermediate features, the depth decoder can act as a
continuously available, label-free teacher for the segmentation branch,
transferring geometric knowledge into the segmentation feature space
without requiring any additional annotations. This is the core
motivation behind the Cross-Task Consistency Loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$
proposed in this project.

\subsection{Feature Alignment Loss Functions: Normalised MSE and Cosine Similarity}
\label{sec:ct-losses}

Implementing cross-task consistency requires a loss function that
quantifies the difference between intermediate features from the two
decoder branches. Comparing raw activation values directly is not
meaningful, since the depth and segmentation branches may operate at
different numerical scales. Both candidate formulations therefore compare
the \emph{direction} of feature vectors rather than their absolute
magnitudes.

Normalised mean squared error (normalised MSE) first applies
$\ell_2$ normalisation to each spatial feature vector, mapping it onto
the unit sphere, then computes the mean squared difference between the
two normalised vectors:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{nMSE}
        = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i}
          \left\|
              \frac{f_\text{seg}^{i}}{\|f_\text{seg}^{i}\|}
              -
              \frac{f_\text{depth}^{i}}{\|f_\text{depth}^{i}\|}
          \right\|^2.
\]
The normalisation step removes the influence of scale differences between
the two branches, so the loss focuses purely on directional alignment.

Cosine similarity loss measures the angle between two vectors directly
and is used in its loss form as $1 - \cos(\cdot,\cdot)$:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{cos}
        = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i}
          \left(
              1
              -
              \frac{f_\text{seg}^{i} \cdot f_\text{depth}^{i}}
                   {\|f_\text{seg}^{i}\|\,\|f_\text{depth}^{i}\|}
          \right).
\]
This value is zero when the two feature vectors point in the same
direction, one when they are orthogonal, and two when they are opposed.
Both formulations work towards aligning the segmentation features with
the depth features, but differ in their gradient behaviour and
sensitivity to outlier activations. Both are included in the
experimental comparison, with results reported in
Chapters~\ref{chap:execution} and~\ref{chap:evaluation}.
