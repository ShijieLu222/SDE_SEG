\chapter{Project Execution}
\label{chap:execution}

This chapter describes the execution of the project and formalises the
proposed Cross-Task Consistency Loss extension introduced at the end of
Chapter~\ref{chap:technical}. The chapter first defines the
label-efficient Cityscapes setting used in this project, including the
labelled and semantically unlabelled subsets. It then specifies how the
proposed $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is integrated into the PAD-MTL pipeline,
gives its mathematical formulation, describes the projection-head designs
evaluated in the experiments, and explains the downstream training
procedure. The final sections summarise the organisation of the
experimental runs and the computing environment used to reproduce and
extend the original framework.

% ============================================================================
\section{Problem Setup and Dataset Configuration}
\label{sec:problem-setup}

This project studies label-efficient semantic segmentation on the
Cityscapes dataset~\cite{cordts2016cityscapes}. Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote
the 2,975-image Cityscapes fine-annotation training set. A labelled subset
$\mathcal{D}_L \subset \mathcal{D}$ of $N_L$ images is selected using the
fixed subset indices provided by the original framework of
Hoyer et al.~\cite{hoyer2023improving}, ensuring comparability with the
original reported results. The remaining images are treated as
semantically unlabelled and form
$\mathcal{D}_U = \mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_L$.

The reproduction experiments cover the annotation budgets of 100, 372, and
744 labelled images, matching the label-budget settings evaluated in the
original framework~\cite{hoyer2023improving}. The proposed Cross-Task
Consistency Loss extension is evaluated primarily under the 372-label
setting, corresponding to the $\nicefrac{1}{8}$ Cityscapes regime. In this
setting, $N_L = 372$ labelled images are used for semantic segmentation
supervision, while the remaining $N_U = 2{,}603$ images are treated as
semantically unlabelled during training.

Each labelled image $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ is paired
with a pixel-level semantic annotation
$y_i \in \{1,\ldots,C\}^{H \times W}$ over $C=19$ classes. For the
self-supervised depth branch, temporally adjacent frames are used when
available to form photometric training triplets
$(x_{t-1}, x_t, x_{t+1})$. These triplets provide photometric supervision
signals for the depth branch without requiring manual depth labels.

The goal is to train a semantic segmentation network
\[
    g_\theta^S \colon \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}
    \to \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W},
\]
which predicts a dense class-logit map for each input image. The final
semantic label map is obtained by applying an argmax operation over the
class dimension.

The model follows the PAD-MTL architecture used in the reproduced
framework. It consists of a shared ResNet-101 encoder $f_\theta^E$, a
semantic segmentation decoder $f_\theta^S$, and a self-supervised depth
decoder $f_\theta^D$. The segmentation and depth branches are therefore
defined as:
\[
    g_\theta^S = f_\theta^S \circ f_\theta^E,
    \qquad
    g_\theta^D = f_\theta^D \circ f_\theta^E.
\]
The shared encoder allows both tasks to use a common visual representation,
while the two task-specific decoders specialise the shared features for
semantic segmentation and depth estimation respectively.

\section{Proposed Extension: Cross-Task Consistency}
\label{sec:proposed-extension}

Chapter~\ref{chap:technical} identified a gap in the PAD-MTL architecture:
its attention-guided distillation module allows the segmentation and depth
branches to exchange information, but this exchange remains implicit. The
model has no direct objective that requires the intermediate segmentation
and depth features to be mutually consistent.

This limitation is especially relevant in the label-efficient setting. The
segmentation branch receives direct semantic supervision only from the
small labelled subset $\mathcal{D}_L$, whereas the depth branch can learn
from photometric reconstruction over temporal image sequences without
requiring manual depth labels. This creates an asymmetry in the available training
signals, motivating the proposed Cross-Task Consistency Loss
$\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$, which introduces an explicit feature-level
alignment objective between the two decoder branches at the PAD
distillation layer.

Unlike the existing PAD feature-exchange module, which passes attended
features between branches, $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ directly penalises
disagreement between intermediate feature representations. The proposed
loss is used only during training. At inference time, the segmentation
prediction is produced by the same segmentation branch as in the PAD-MTL
baseline, and the projection heads introduced for $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$
are not required.

\section{Formulation of \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$}{Lct}}
\label{sec:lct-formulation}

\subsection{Feature Representations}
\label{sec:feature-repr}

In this project's PAD configuration, the cross-task consistency loss is
applied at the PAD distillation layer, corresponding to decoder upsampling
layer~7 in this implementation. At this layer, the depth and segmentation
decoder branches produce feature maps with spatial resolution
$128 \times 128$ and $C_d = 128$ channels.

Let
\[
    f_\text{depth}
    \in
    \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_d \times H_d \times W_d},
    \qquad
    f_\text{seg}
    \in
    \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_d \times H_d \times W_d}
\]
denote the depth and segmentation features at this layer for a minibatch
of size $B$, where $H_d = W_d = 128$.

The loss is applied at the feature level rather than the output level
because the final task outputs have incompatible meanings: semantic
segmentation produces class logits, while depth estimation produces a
geometric depth prediction. Aligning intermediate feature representations
therefore provides a more appropriate way to encourage cross-task
consistency without forcing one task output to imitate the other.

Figure~\ref{fig:ct-loss-architecture} illustrates where these intermediate
features are extracted and how $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is attached to the
PAD-MTL architecture across the three projection layouts.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/cross_task_consistency_extension.drawio.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture-level placement of the proposed
    $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ within PAD-MTL. The shared encoder
    (SDE \texttt{fd2}-initialised) feeds two task-specific decoder branches for
    semantic segmentation and self-supervised depth estimation.
    Intermediate features $f_\text{seg}$ and $f_\text{depth}$ are
    extracted at the PAD distillation layer. Depending on the selected
    layout, one or two lightweight $1\!\times\!1$ projection heads are
    used to produce comparable feature representations, and
    $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is computed between them using either
    normalised MSE or cosine similarity. Three projection layouts are
    supported: single $(d\!\rightarrow\!s)$, single $(s\!\rightarrow\!d)$,
    and dual projection. The projection head(s) are used only during
    training and are not required at inference time.}
    \label{fig:ct-loss-architecture}
\end{figure}

The following subsections formalise the projection head and the two
distance functions used to instantiate $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$.

\subsection{Projection Head}
\label{sec:proj-head}

Although $f_\text{depth}$ and $f_\text{seg}$ have the same spatial
resolution and number of channels, they are produced by different
task-specific decoders. Directly forcing them to match may be too
restrictive, because each branch is expected to retain task-specific
information. Therefore, a lightweight learnable projection head is used to
map one branch's features into a space comparable with the other branch.

The projection head is implemented as a $1 \times 1$ convolution:
\[
    \phi \colon \mathbb{R}^{C_d} \to \mathbb{R}^{C_d}.
\]
It preserves the spatial resolution and channel dimensionality of the
input feature map, applying a learned linear transformation independently
at each spatial position. With $C_d=128$, one projection head contains
$128^2 = 16{,}384$ weight parameters and $128$ bias parameters, giving
$16{,}512$ additional parameters per head.

Depending on the projection layout, either one or two such projection
heads are used. The projection layouts are described in
Section~\ref{sec:projection-design}.

\subsection{Loss Formulations}
\label{sec:loss-forms}

Two scale-insensitive alignment objectives are evaluated: normalised mean
squared error and cosine similarity loss. Both compare feature directions
rather than raw activation magnitudes, because the depth and segmentation
decoders may naturally operate at different numerical scales.

\paragraph{Normalised MSE.}
Given a projected representation
$z \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C_d \times H_d \times W_d}$ and a target
feature representation $t$ of the same shape, define the channel-wise
$\ell_2$-normalised vectors:
\[
    \hat{z}_{b,p}
    =
    \frac{z_{b,p}}{\|z_{b,p}\|_2 + \varepsilon},
    \qquad
    \hat{t}_{b,p}
    =
    \frac{t_{b,p}}{\|t_{b,p}\|_2 + \varepsilon},
\]
where $p$ indexes spatial positions and $\varepsilon > 0$ is a small
constant for numerical stability. The normalised MSE loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{MSE}(z,t)
    =
    \frac{1}{B H_d W_d}
    \sum_{b=1}^{B}
    \sum_{p=1}^{H_d W_d}
    \left\|
    \hat{z}_{b,p}
    -
    \hat{t}_{b,p}
    \right\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:lct-mse}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Cosine similarity loss.}
The cosine similarity loss penalises angular disagreement between
corresponding spatial feature vectors:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{cos}(z,t)
    =
    1
    -
    \frac{1}{B H_d W_d}
    \sum_{b=1}^{B}
    \sum_{p=1}^{H_d W_d}
    \frac{
        z_{b,p} \cdot t_{b,p}
    }{
        \|z_{b,p}\|_2 \|t_{b,p}\|_2 + \varepsilon
    }.
    \label{eq:lct-cos}
\end{equation}
Both objectives are minimised when the compared feature vectors point in
the same direction, yielding zero for perfectly aligned vectors. The cosine
loss saturates at~$2$ for antipodal vectors; the normalised MSE reaches a
maximum of~$4$ under the same condition.

\subsection{Definition of the Cross-Task Consistency Loss}
\label{sec:lct-def}

For a given projection layout, the Cross-Task Consistency Loss
$\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is computed using either the normalised MSE loss
or the cosine similarity loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{ct}
    =
    \mathcal{L}(z,t),
    \qquad
    \mathcal{L}
    \in
    \left\{
    \mathcal{L}_\text{MSE},\;
    \mathcal{L}_\text{cos}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:lct}
\end{equation}
The specific pair of feature representations $(z,t)$ compared by
$\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ depends on the projection layout described in
Section~\ref{sec:projection-design}. The scalar weight
$\lambda_\text{ct}$ is applied only once in the final composite objective,
so that $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ denotes the loss itself and
$\lambda_\text{ct}$ controls its relative strength during optimisation.

The candidate values
$\lambda_\text{ct} \in \{0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00\}$ are evaluated
empirically in the calibration experiments presented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Projection Head Design}
\label{sec:projection-design}

Three projection layouts are evaluated. They differ in which branch is
projected and whether the alignment is performed in one branch's
unprojected feature space or in a shared projected space. The reference
branch is not frozen; it simply remains unprojected in the loss
computation. In the equations below, $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the selected
distance function, either $\mathcal{L}_\text{MSE}$ or
$\mathcal{L}_\text{cos}$.

\subsection{Single Projection: \texorpdfstring{$d \rightarrow s$}{d to s}}
\label{sec:single-dtos}

In the depth-to-segmentation layout, the depth feature is projected:
\[
    z = \phi(f_\text{depth}).
\]
The loss is computed between the projected depth representation and the
unprojected segmentation feature:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{ct}^{d\rightarrow s}
    =
    \mathcal{L}(z,\; f_\text{seg}).
\]
This layout maps depth features into the segmentation feature space. The
unprojected segmentation feature provides the reference space for the
alignment, although gradients are still allowed to update both branches.
The intuition is that geometric information from the depth branch may help
regularise the segmentation branch while preserving the segmentation
feature space as the alignment reference.

\subsection{Single Projection: \texorpdfstring{$s \rightarrow d$}{s to d}}
\label{sec:single-stod}

In the segmentation-to-depth layout, the segmentation feature is projected:
\[
    z = \phi(f_\text{seg}).
\]
The loss is computed between the projected segmentation representation and
the unprojected depth feature:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{ct}^{s\rightarrow d}
    =
    \mathcal{L}(z,\; f_\text{depth}).
\]
Here, the unprojected depth feature provides the reference space for the
alignment, although it is not frozen during optimisation. This layout
encourages the segmentation branch to produce intermediate features that
are compatible with the geometric representation learned by the depth
branch.

\subsection{Dual Projection}
\label{sec:dual-proj}

In the dual-projection layout, both branches are projected using separate
projection heads:
\[
    z_d = \phi_d(f_\text{depth}),
    \qquad
    z_s = \phi_s(f_\text{seg}).
\]
The loss is computed between the two projected representations:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{ct}^{\text{dual}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}(z_d,\; z_s).
\]
This introduces a symmetric alignment constraint in which both feature
spaces are allowed to adapt simultaneously. It doubles the additional
parameter count to $33{,}024$. Unlike the single-projection layouts, no
branch's original feature space serves as the direct reference. Instead,
both branches are encouraged to meet in a shared projected space. This may
increase flexibility, but it may also remove the stable anchor provided by
an unprojected reference branch.

The relative effectiveness of these projection layouts is evaluated
empirically in Section~\ref{sec:ct-calibration-ablation}.

\label{sec:training-pipeline}

    \label{fig:training-pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Pre-training Stage: Self-Supervised Depth Estimation}
\label{sec:pretraining-stage}

Before downstream segmentation training, the shared encoder $f_\theta^E$
and depth decoder $f_\theta^D$ are pre-trained on image sequences using
the self-supervised depth estimation objective $\mathcal{L}_D$. This
objective combines photometric reconstruction with a pose estimation
network that predicts relative camera motion between adjacent frames.

The \texttt{fd2} checkpoint used throughout this project is obtained by
adding a feature-distance regularisation term during SDE pre-training:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{D,\text{pretrain}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_D
    +
    \lambda_F \mathcal{L}_F,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{L}_F
    =
    \left\|
    f_\theta^E - f_I^E
    \right\|_2,
    \label{eq:pretrain-loss}
\end{equation}
where $f_I^E$ denotes the frozen ImageNet-initialised encoder. The role of
$\mathcal{L}_F$ is to prevent the encoder from drifting too far away from
the ImageNet feature space during depth pre-training. This helps retain
semantic representations that are useful for downstream segmentation while
still incorporating geometric cues from SDE.

The segmentation decoder is not trained during this pre-training stage.
The resulting \texttt{fd2} encoder weights are then used to initialise the
downstream PAD-MTL model, where the encoder remains trainable.

\subsection{Downstream Stage: Joint Segmentation and Multi-Task Training}
\label{sec:downstream-stage}

After SDE pre-training, the PAD-MTL model is trained jointly for semantic
segmentation and depth estimation. The segmentation branch is supervised
using the labelled subset $\mathcal{D}_L$. The depth branch continues to
receive self-supervised geometric supervision from image sequences.
When semi-supervised components such as DepthMix are enabled, unlabelled
or pseudo-labelled samples also contribute to the segmentation objective.

During this downstream stage, $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is applied at the
PAD distillation layer. At each training iteration, the model performs the
following operations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item sample labelled images and, where applicable, unlabelled or
    pseudo-labelled images;
    \item apply the configured augmentations, including DepthMix when
    enabled;
    \item forward the images through the shared encoder and both decoder
    branches;
    \item compute the segmentation loss, the self-supervised depth loss,
    and any semi-supervised segmentation contribution;
    \item extract $f_\text{seg}$ and $f_\text{depth}$ from the PAD
    distillation layer;
    \item apply the selected projection layout to obtain the projected
    representation(s);
    \item compute $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ using the selected loss
    formulation;
    \item optimise the composite objective defined in
    Eq.~\ref{eq:total-loss}.
\end{enumerate}

The downstream training stage runs for 40,000 iterations using SGD
optimisation, following the reproduced framework.

\subsection{Composite Training Objective}
\label{sec:composite-objective}

The downstream training objective is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{total}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_\text{seg}
    +
    \mathcal{L}_D
    +
    \lambda_\text{ct}\mathcal{L}_\text{ct},
    \label{eq:total-loss}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\mathcal{L}_\text{seg}$ is the full segmentation objective,
    comprising the supervised cross-entropy loss on the labelled subset
    $\mathcal{D}_L$ and, when semi-supervised components are enabled,
    the quality-weighted cross-entropy on pseudo-labelled or
    DepthMix-augmented samples;
    \item $\mathcal{L}_D$ is the self-supervised depth estimation loss
    computed from image sequences;
    \item $\lambda_\text{ct}\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ is the proposed
    cross-task feature alignment term.
\end{enumerate}

The feature-distance loss $\mathcal{L}_F$ does not appear in
Eq.~\ref{eq:total-loss}. It is used only during the SDE pre-training stage
to produce the \texttt{fd2} initialisation, as shown in
Eq.~\ref{eq:pretrain-loss}, and is not continued during downstream joint
training.

\subsection{Gradient Flow and Parameter Groups}
\label{sec:grad-flow}

In all projection layouts, gradients from $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ flow
bidirectionally. They pass through the projection head into whichever
branch's features are projected, and also flow directly into the
unprojected branch. This means that the unprojected feature acts as a
reference space in the loss computation, but it is not frozen during
optimisation.

This design is intentional. The goal is not to treat one branch as a fixed
teacher and the other as a student. Instead, $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ acts
as a regulariser that encourages both decoder branches to learn more
compatible intermediate representations.

The projection head parameters are assigned to the decoders and
auxiliary heads parameter group and updated at the decoder learning rate
of $10^{-2}$.
This applies to the single-projection head $\phi$ in the single-layout
variants and to both $\phi_d$ and $\phi_s$ in the dual-projection layout.
The backbone encoder is updated at $10^{-3}$ and the pose network at
$10^{-6}$, following the reproduced training configuration.

\section{Computing Environment and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:computing-environment}

The reproduction experiments, corresponding to exp~210--213, were run on
the University of Bristol's Isambard-AI platform. The subsequent
cross-task consistency loss experiments, corresponding to exp~219,
exp~220, and exp~222, were run on the University of Bristol's BluePebble
high-performance computing platform under the SLURM workload manager.

All experiments used a single GPU per job. On BluePebble, each job was
allocated one GPU, eight physical CPU cores, and 64\,GB of system memory.
The extension experiments were run predominantly on V100 nodes with
32\,GB of GPU memory. All runs used a dedicated Conda environment with
pinned dependencies to ensure consistent behaviour across node types.

Random seeds were fixed across Python, NumPy, and PyTorch at the start of
each run. Each experiment configuration, including experiment ID, label
budget, seed, projection layout, loss type, and $\lambda_\text{ct}$
value, was logged alongside model checkpoints and validation results.

A key practical constraint was that all experiments were run on a single
GPU. As a result, the effective batch size was limited to~2 and
Synchronised Batch Normalisation could not be used. This differs from the
multi-GPU setup of the original framework, where batch-normalisation
statistics were synchronised across devices. The impact of this constraint
is considered in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} when interpreting the
reproduction gap between this project and the original reported results.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:exec-summary}

This chapter has described the project execution and formalised the
proposed Cross-Task Consistency Loss extension. The chapter first defined
the Cityscapes label-efficient setting, where a 372-image labelled subset
is used together with semantically unlabelled images and temporal image
sequences for self-supervised depth estimation. It then introduced the
proposed explicit feature-level alignment objective between the
segmentation and depth decoder branches of PAD-MTL.

The proposed $\mathcal{L}_\text{ct}$ was formulated as a feature-level
alignment loss applied at the PAD distillation layer. Two loss variants
were defined: normalised MSE and cosine similarity. Three projection
layouts were described: single $d\rightarrow s$, single $s\rightarrow d$,
and dual projection. The loss was then integrated into the downstream
training objective as
\[
    \mathcal{L}_\text{total}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_\text{seg}
    +
    \mathcal{L}_D
    +
    \lambda_\text{ct}\mathcal{L}_\text{ct},
\]
where $\mathcal{L}_\text{seg}$ covers both the supervised cross-entropy
loss on $\mathcal{D}_L$ and any semi-supervised segmentation contribution
from pseudo-labelled or DepthMix-augmented samples.

The chapter also described the experiment organisation, computing
environment, and reproducibility controls. The quantitative evaluation of
the reproduction experiments and the proposed cross-task consistency
extension is presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.
